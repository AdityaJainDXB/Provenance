Why we built this

Against counterfeit culture

Traditional craft is being copied faster than it can be made. Factory versions of village pottery, machine-loomed "handwoven" cloth, motifs lifted from communities that see none of the money. They flood the market, undercut the people who hold the knowledge, and slowly make the real thing look overpriced. Provenance is a narrow, deliberate response.

The premise

An object is worth more when you know where it came from

Not as a marketing line — structurally. If you can name the maker, watch the technique, and confirm the region, a piece stops being a commodity and becomes a specific thing made by a specific person. Counterfeits can copy the look. They can't copy the record.

So we made the record the product. Every listing is anchored to a verified artisan and their story. Nothing goes up without one.

A marketplace that reads like a gallery, and pays like a fair one.
— The founding principle
What we hold to

Five commitments

I

Every piece has a named maker

No anonymous stock, no house brands, no resellers standing between you and the workshop.

II

Authenticity is verified, not claimed

Process video, cooperative endorsement, region of origin — checked by people, reviewed yearly.

III

Visibility is shared

Rotation pauses at 500 daily sales or 50 high-rated feed placements — so no single maker can dominate the gallery or the feed. When one fills up, another is surfaced.

IV

New makers get a guaranteed shelf

Under-resourced and rural artisans start in their own rotation with 1,000 guaranteed feed impressions, seen before they have a single rating.

V

The pace is slow on purpose

Pagination, not infinite scroll. "Acquire", not "add to cart". Considered buying is better for makers and buyers both.

VI

Ratings serve the craft, not the ego

One private 10-star score per acquisition — material quality, cultural authenticity, craft execution. No public review wall, no popularity metric.

Who this is for

Two audiences, one interest

Buyers anywhere in the world who want cultural goods that are actually what they say they are — ethically sourced, traceably made, bought in a way that respects the object. Artisan communities, including under-resourced and rural makers, who hold traditional knowledge and need fair visibility and reliable income from it — not a platform that buries them beneath whoever can post most often.
